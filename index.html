What makes a first app succeed

Keep the scope to one skill

The apps that work are small. One skill, one kind of activity, one sitting of about five to ten minutes.

  • Too big: an app that teaches the whole unit on cell division.
  • Right size: a drill where students put the phases of mitosis in order and get told which pair they swapped.

You can always add to it later. A small app that works beats a large one that half works.

Put your effort into the wrong-answer feedback

Telling a student they are wrong teaches nothing. Telling them which part went wrong teaches the skill.

  • Name the specific error: "Your units didn't cancel. Look at the milligrams."
  • Give a hint, then let them try again, rather than showing the answer at once.
  • Write different feedback for the common wrong answers you already see in class.
Expect to iterate, and change one thing at a time

Vibe coding is a conversation, not a single request. Plan on five to fifteen rounds of "change this, fix that."

  • Ask for one change per message. Several at once makes it hard to see what broke.
  • Save a copy of each version that works before asking for a big change. Number them: app-v1.html, app-v2.html.
  • Describe problems concretely: "The Next button does nothing after question 3" beats "it's broken." Paste in any error message you see.
  • Describe behavior, not code. "When a student answers wrong, show a hint and let them retry" is all the AI needs.
You are the expert; the AI is the typist

AI writes confident prose whether or not it is correct. It will invent plausible drug doses, dates, citations, and formulas.

  • Check every question, answer key, explanation, and piece of feedback yourself before students see it.
  • Supply your own content where accuracy matters. Paste your problems in rather than asking the AI to invent them.
  • Ask it to explain its work: "Explain what this code does in plain language" and "Does this app send any information anywhere?"
Use content you are allowed to use

An open educational resource has to be clean on rights all the way down.

  • Safest sources: material you wrote, public domain works, and openly licensed material such as OpenStax or other CC-licensed resources.
  • Not safe: publisher test banks, figures scanned from a textbook, and images pulled from a web search.
  • Keep a note of each source and its license. You will need it when you publish.
  • CC BY 4.0 is a common default: others may reuse and adapt your app as long as they credit you.

        Saving and keeping track of versions
        

          
AI tools lose track of earlier versions. Keep your own copies.

          
    
            
  • Save the code to a file whenever it works. Name files in order: app-v1.html, app-v2.html.
    • 
            
  • To save: copy the code, paste it into a plain text editor (Notepad or TextEdit in plain text mode), and save it with an .html ending.
    • 
            
  • To test: double-click the file. It opens in your browser and runs.
    • 
            
  • If a change makes things worse, open your last good file and paste it back into the chat: "Go back to this version and make this change instead."

        What the scan can and cannot tell you
        

          
The scan looks for text patterns. It catches the common problems, but it can miss something and it can flag harmless code. It is a first pass, not an approval.

          
It cannot judge whether your content is accurate, whether the activity teaches what you intended, or whether a screen reader actually makes sense of the app. Only you and a human reviewer can do that.

        

      

      

        Why "no outside connections" matters
        

          
If an app sends anything to another server, student work may leave your institution's control, which raises FERPA questions and campus review requirements.

          
    
            
  • A self-contained file avoids this entirely: nothing leaves the student's browser.
    • 
            
  • Watch for fetch, XMLHttpRequest, a <form> with an action, and script or image tags pointing at http addresses.
    • 
            
  • Never collect names, student IDs, or email addresses. Let the LMS handle identity, and let the app handle practice.
    • 
            
  • If something must be saved, keep it in the student's own browser and store nothing identifying.
    • 
          

        

      

      

        Accessibility: the quick version
        

          
Accessibility is a legal obligation and the difference between an app some students can use and one every student can use.

          
    
            
  • Keyboard: put your mouse aside and press Tab. You should be able to reach and operate everything, and always see where you are.
    • 
            
  • Announcements: feedback that appears after an answer should be announced, usually with aria-live.
    • 
            
  • Color: never let color be the only signal. Add a word or an icon next to the green and the red.
    • 
            
  • Contrast and text: at least 4.5:1, and the layout should survive zooming to 200%.
    • 
            
  • Images and media: alt text on images, captions or transcripts for audio and video.
    • 
            
  • Check it: free tools such as WAVE or axe DevTools catch much of this in seconds.
    • 
          

        

      

      

        Putting it in your LMS
        

          
    
            
  • Save the finished code as a file ending in .html.
    • 
            
  • Upload it to a sandbox or practice course first, not your live course.
    • 
            
  • Open it in student view and test it on both a computer and a phone.
    • 
            
  • Then add it to your live course as a content item and check student view once more before releasing it.
    • 
            
  • Some systems restrict what uploaded pages may do. If it works on your computer but not in the LMS, ask your teaching center or LMS administrator.
